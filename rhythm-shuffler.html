See exactly where the timing slipped

Each note takes the colour of its timing, and a dot under it shifts towards the start or end of the bar to show how early or late you were. A miss shows a cross, and the colours are chosen to stay clear with colour blindness.

Tap any note to see how far off it was in milliseconds. If most of your taps were early or late, a short tip tells you which.

The first time you use a speaker or headphones, a quick 15-second calibration learns their timing, so your taps are judged fairly.

From Starter to Master

Six difficulty settings, each adding one new idea, so you can move on when the last one feels easy.

  1. Starter

    One rhythm, repeated, with a note on every beat.

  2. Easy

    A second rhythm joins in, along with your first rests.

  3. Normal

    Richer rhythms, with rests anywhere.

  4. Hard

    Your first syncopation: notes that start off the beat.

  5. Expert

    Adds semiquavers, triplets and ties.

  6. Master

    The widest mix of rhythms, arranged freely.

Reading rhythms with Rhythm Shuffler

How to use it, the habits I teach, common note values, key terms and common questions.

Who it helps

Reading rhythm is a separate skill from reading pitch, and it is the one my students most often skip. Rhythm Shuffler helps:

  • Students who can count a rhythm but freeze when they see it written down.
  • Beginners meeting quavers, rests and dotted notes for the first time.
  • Students preparing for sight-reading in exams or ensemble rehearsals.
  • Teachers who need new rhythms quickly, without writing them out by hand.

How to use it

Choose a difficulty, a time signature and a tempo, then shuffle to get a rhythm. Read it, tap it back on the screen in time, and check how accurate you were.

  1. Choose one of the six difficulty settings.
  2. Set the time signature, the tempo and the number of bars.
  3. Shuffle to get a new rhythm, then start it.
  4. Tap the rhythm on the screen as you read it.
  5. Check the result: your accuracy as a percentage and how many notes were on the beat, early, late or missed. Then try the same rhythm again or shuffle a new one.

Practice tips from a teacher

The rhythms can include dotted notes, rests and ties. These habits help my students read them:

  • Count the beat out loud for a bar before you tap the first note.
  • Look one beat ahead, not at the note you are tapping.
  • Treat rests as part of the rhythm and keep counting through them.
  • When a rhythm goes wrong, try it again before you shuffle a new one.

Key terms

Rhythm
The pattern of long and short sounds and silences in music, set against a steady beat.
Syncopation
Placing accents where you would not normally expect them, often off the beat, which gives the music a push.
Tie
A curved line joining two notes of the same pitch, so they sound as one note lasting for both values.
Crotchet
A note worth one beat in 4/4 time. In American English it is called a quarter note.

Questions

Do I need to read music to use Rhythm Shuffler?

You need to know the basic note values, such as crotchets and quavers. The easier settings use simpler rhythms, so it is a good way to learn the rest.

How does Rhythm Shuffler measure my timing?

When you finish tapping, it shows your accuracy as a percentage and how many notes were on the beat, early, late or missed, so you can see exactly where the timing slipped.

Will I see the same rhythms again?

The rhythms are fresh each time, so you practise reading rather than memorising one pattern.
